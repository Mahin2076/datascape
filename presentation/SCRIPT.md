# Speaker script

Two sentences per slide. Speaker 1 takes slides 1–5, Speaker 2 takes 6–10, Speaker 3 takes 11–14.
Arrow keys move between slides; `n` shows the notes.

## Speaker 1 (slides 1–5)

1. **DataScape.** We wanted to know what a rent number actually means for the people paying it. So we built a way to go looking.
2. **Rent is usually a number on a chart.** San Francisco's median rent has climbed to $2,476 a month. That's a real number, but it's hard to picture anyone behind it.
3. **So we put it on a street.** Click anywhere in San Francisco and DataScape builds that block from real map data. The roads, parks and courts are where they really are, and people walk around them.
4. **This is Tasha.** Tasha runs a bakery and has rented on this street for nine years, with a teenage kid at home. Tasha isn't a real person, but the rent and income come from San Francisco's Census numbers.
5. **Rent is $2,724 a month.** The household brings in about $8,200 a month. So rent takes about a third of it.

## Speaker 2 (slides 6–10)

6. **Next year, rent goes up 10%.** That's $272 more every month for Tasha. It has to come out of something else in the budget.
7. **Tasha isn't unusual.** About 4 in 10 San Francisco renters spend 30% or more of their income on rent. So of the ten people walking near Tasha, about four are in the same spot.
8. **So you can ask the whole block.** In the app, you type a question and the people nearby answer in their own words. Each bar shows how much of that household's income goes to rent after a 10% increase.
9. **Does a 5% raise close the gap?** In dollars, yes: all four households keep a bit more money than before. But rent still takes a bigger share of every paycheck than it did before the increase.
10. **It works anywhere in the city.** Here we jump across town to the west end of Golden Gate Park. The soccer fields, paths and trees line up with the real satellite photo.

## Speaker 3 (slides 11–14)

11. **Three kinds of data, one place.** We stack satellite photos, OpenStreetMap roads and buildings, and Census rent and income numbers. They all line up on the same real spot.
12. **In New York, it's over half.** We compared five cities, and in New York 52% of renters spend 30% or more of their income on rent. San Francisco is the lowest at 39%, because incomes there are high.
13. **The data surprised us.** We expected rent to be growing faster than income everywhere. In most cities the difference is too small to call, and in New York incomes actually grew faster.
14. **Can you afford to stay?** Click anywhere in San Francisco, meet who lives there, and ask them. Thank you!
